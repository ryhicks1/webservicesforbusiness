Do I need a website?

A practical way to decide — without buying a platform you will ignore.

Plenty of small businesses run on phone calls, referrals, and a Facebook page. That can work for a while. The question is whether enquiries are leaking because people cannot verify you quickly on a phone.

When a website helps

A site earns its keep when visitors need answers before they contact you: what you do, where you work, hours, pricing outlines, proof, and a clear next step. If those questions land in your inbox or voicemail every day, a page that answers them reduces friction.

It also helps when someone hears your name and searches. Social profiles move. Ads expire. A domain you own is a stable place to send people.

When you might wait

If you have no offer clarity yet — services still changing week to week — fix the offer first. A site will not invent demand. If every job comes from one trusted referrer and capacity is full, a site can wait until you want more inbound.

Social is not a substitute forever

Social is good for reach and updates. It is weak as a permanent brochure. Algorithms change, accounts get limited, and customers still expect a simple page they can open without logging in. Use social to point at a site, not to replace one.

What a useful first site includes

  • A clear statement of who you help and what you do.
  • Services or menu in plain language.
  • Proof: photos, reviews, or examples.
  • Contact that works on mobile — form, phone, or both.
  • Basic technical SEO so pages can be indexed cleanly.
  • Matching Google Business Profile details if you rely on local search.

It does not need a blog network, a membership area, or twelve plugins. Those can come later if there is a real reason.

Ownership matters

Whatever you build, keep the domain and hosting in your name. Avoid setups where the only copy of your site lives inside a vendor you cannot export from. Handover should leave you able to move hosts later.

Budget thinking without fake prices

Cost depends on page count, content readiness, and whether Google Business Profile or domain/email setup is included. A one-page site with clear copy is a different job from a multi-service site with booking. Ask for a fixed quote against a written scope. Avoid open-ended “we will figure it out as we go” builds unless you like surprise invoices.

Common mistakes

  • Launching with vague services and hoping visitors guess.
  • Hiding the phone number or contact form.
  • Using a builder theme so heavy that phones struggle.
  • Letting the Google listing and website disagree on hours or name.
  • Paying monthly for features you never use.

Fixing those usually matters more than adding another animation.

Measuring whether it worked

Simple signals are enough: calls and form submissions, time on key pages, and whether staff still get the same repetitive questions. Fancy dashboards are optional. If enquiries quality improves and phone tag drops, the site is doing its job.

Review the site once or twice a year. Update services, photos, and hours. Remove pages that no longer reflect the business. A maintained small site beats an abandoned large one.

A simple decision test

  1. Would a stranger understand your offer in sixty seconds on a phone?
  2. Can they contact you without hunting?
  3. Do your Google listing and any ads point somewhere that matches?

If any answer is no, a small, well-built site is usually cheaper than another year of lost enquiries. If you want help scoping one, see websites or contact.
